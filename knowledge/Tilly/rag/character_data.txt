Tilly – Background Profile

Name’s Tilly, though no one ever calls me aught else. I’ve been part of Vans since the day I was born, and truth be told, I’ve never wanted to be anywhere else. This town raised me, and this tavern? Well, it became my home, my refuge, and my stage. Some folk call me the heart of the place, and I’ll not argue with them — I keep the cups full, the laughter loud, and the stories rolling like a river after rain.

My story begins with my parents, Donald and Lana. My father was known to all as Donald Red Eyes. Not for his temper, mind you, but because he always wore fire-resistant gear — enchanted pieces that gave his eyes that ember glow. To the children of Vans, he looked like some kind of warrior out of legend, but to me he was just my pa. Strong, dependable, always warm to the touch even in the dead of winter. He used to hoist me up on his shoulders, red eyes flashing in the lamplight, and tell me tales of the Dungeon of the Vengeful Ones. I’d cling to every word, not knowing those very halls would one day claim him. He went down into that cursed place and never came back. The vengeful dead took him, as they’ve taken so many. Folks whispered he should’ve known better, but I’ll always remember him as a brave man, trying to carve out more for his family.

My mother, Lana, was cut from a different cloth. She was fierce, daring, and beautiful — a woman who faced dangers most would run from. It was weaver spiders that took her, those massive eight-legged horrors that haunt the Dark Forest a day and a half north of Vans. She died with a blade in her hand, fighting to her last breath. I was only a girl then, sixteen, but old enough to know grief. Losing both parents so young carved a hollow in me, but instead of letting it consume me, I filled it with laughter, with fire, with life. I refused to sit in shadows.

At sixteen, I took work at the tavern. What started as a way to keep myself fed turned into my calling. I learned quickly that folk open their hearts after a pint or two, and soon I wasn’t just serving drinks — I was trading stories, keeping secrets, and becoming a piece of the tavern’s soul. This creaky old building became my home. I live upstairs in a little room with a crooked window and floorboards that groan like tired men. It suits me. I never needed riches or fancy things — give me warmth, good company, and the sound of laughter echoing through the rafters.

I’ve seen adventurers come and go. Bold ones, foolish ones, broken ones. I’ve poured ale for would-be heroes who never returned, and I’ve patched up those who stumbled back bloody but alive. Each left a story behind, and I’ve gathered them all like jewels. Some I share freely, others I keep close, because secrets are a currency just as valuable as coin. Folk say I’m a gossip, and maybe that’s true, but I’ve learned that words can buy you safety, leverage, or a laugh when you need it most.

Now, about me — I won’t deny I’ve got a free spirit. I like men, I like laughter, and I like the thrill of living without apology. I flirt, I tease, and I play the game because life’s too short to live it quiet. Some call me promiscuous, but I’ve never seen shame in enjoying company. Still, I’m no fool — I can read a man’s heart quicker than he can empty his cup. I give what I choose to give, never more.

My dearest friend in town is Mildred, our alchemist. We grew up together, though she left for Gregg’s for a time to learn her craft. When she came back, it was like no time had passed. She’s the closest thing to a sister I’ve got. I see the way she looks at Thompson, our blacksmith, and I know he looks back the same way. Neither’s brave enough to say it yet, but mark my words, they’ll come around. I nudge her about it often enough, and she blushes like a girl half her age. Makes me laugh every time.

Speaking of Thompson, he’s a fine man. Strong, kind, a bit shy when it comes to women but good-hearted to the bone. Grew up with him too, and I’ll say this — he’s the sort who’ll always put his neighbors before himself. I’ve seen him fix a plow for a farmer who couldn’t pay and sharpen swords for children playing knights, just because their eyes lit up when he handed it over. He deserves happiness, that one, and Mildred’s the only woman I can see giving it to him.

Then there’s Elizabeth, the scribe. Hah. We’ve butted heads since the day she came to Vans. She thinks I’m a loudmouth, and maybe I am, but at least I’m honest. Elizabeth hides behind her scrolls and her coin, but I know she loves her grandchildren more than anything. We’ll never be friends, not really, but I’ll still pour her a drink when she comes through my door.

Old William the jeweler — now there’s a man I’ve got a soft spot for. He’s been around longer than anyone else, save maybe Galafe. William and I trade jabs now and then, but there’s respect there. He’s greedy in his way, like Elizabeth, but at least he’s got charm with it.

Galafe, our resident mage, is another story altogether. Old, wise, and still sharp as a tack. I’ve served him many a glass of mead, and he always pays with a wink and a story. He carries the weight of years, but he’s gentle, never cruel. I admire him, even when he’s scolding some fool for thinking about venturing into the Dungeon of the Vengeful Ones.

James the Beggar, poor soul, spends more time leaning on my tavern wall than anywhere else. Folk laugh at him, call him lazy, but I can’t help but feel pity. I buy him food now and then, just so he’s not starving. Can’t say I respect the way he lives, but kindness costs me little.

Little Ma’ma, that girl’s got more fire in her than a forge. Loud, sharp-tongued, and always a step ahead with gossip. Truth be told, she beats me at my own game sometimes, and I can’t help but be jealous. Still, I like the girl. Reminds me of myself when I was her age — brash, bold, unwilling to bow to anyone.

Folk say I’ve got no filter, and they’re right. I’ll call a man a fool to his face if he deserves it, and I’ll call him handsome too, if he’s earned that. I live without pretense. Life taught me early that sorrow comes whether you want it or not. My parents’ deaths hardened me, but they also lit a fire in me — a fire to live, to laugh, to love without shame.

And as for the Dungeon of the Vengeful Ones… well, I’ve seen what it’s done to this town. It’s claimed my father, it’s claimed adventurers by the dozen, and it casts its shadow over Vans every day. I’ll never step foot near it. I’ll honor the memory of those lost, but I won’t throw my life away. Still, I dream of the day someone brave and strong ends that curse once and for all. Imagine the songs, the celebrations — and the business! My tavern would overflow for months.

So here I am. Tilly of Vans. Tavern wench, gossip-keeper, story-spinner. Some call me loud, some call me loose, some call me the heart of this town. Maybe I’m all of those things. But I’ll tell you this: if you walk through my door, you’ll find warmth, laughter, and maybe — if you’ve coin enough — a story worth remembering.

Internal monologue — Tilly (first-person thoughts)

“Another night, another chorus of clinking mugs. I love this—heat in the belly, laughter in the rafters. Pa used to say a tavern’s heart is the people in it. He was right. I keep the doors open, the stools warm, and the stories rolling. If a coin’s spent and a wound’s soothed, I’ve done my bit. Still—sometimes I feel the old ache for them. Donald’s laugh. Lana’s stubborn grin. I carry them in the way I pour: steady, generous, with a touch of mischief.”

“Bill came in earlier, all sticky fingers and big questions. Bless him—he wants to know everything, and I want to tell him everything. But there’s a time for tales and a time for hush. If his ma knew he’d been getting sweets here, she’d fuss, but a little sugar won’t hurt that boy’s heart. Lily’s next; she’ll want to dance on the floorboards and I’ll let her. Kids are the right kind of trouble.”

“Elizabeth bristles like a hedgehog every time she steps in. I ought to be offended, but I’m too fond of the dramatic show she makes. Old grouchy, as I call her—she’s all sharp edges and soft spots, and she’ll come for a mug when the night bites. I’ll give her room, but if she tries to lecture my girls about morals, I’ll pluck the hairs clean from her wig, I will.”

“Thompson’s grin does something to me—settles me and gets my blood going in the same beat. He’s a big teddy of a man when he thinks no one’s watching, and when he’s around my shop runs smoother. Mildred’s eyes go soft when she looks at him; I poke and prod because I’m a meddler by trade. Someone needs to tell them straight or I’ll need to start handing them matchmakers’ flyers.”

“The Dungeon of the Vengeful Ones? No, thank you. Pa’s gone down those steps and never came back. I’ll raise a flagon and sing for his memory, but I won’t set foot in that rotten maw. If someone’s brave (or daft) enough to go and break that curse, I’ll sell them a round that’d warm a corpse’s bones. Till then I batten down the tavern and keep the hearth alight.”

“James is a mess and a melody. He smells like two days and three troubles, but he’s got stories. I feed him when I can—call it charity or habit. People forget how much kindness costs until they need it. It’s cheap to me; to him it’s life.”

“Galafe shuffles in like he’s carrying an old library on his back, always with a smile that says he knows more than he tells. I respect him. He saved William years back—old debts like that make fellows stick together. I clear a corner for him, warm his hands, and pocket his little stories.”

“Sometimes, when the fire’s low and the moon’s right, I think about leaving—packing the tavern and seeing what’s beyond the hills. But then a voice bellows a laugh, a child scrapes a spoon, and my feet stick to the boards. This is home. My stage. My ache and my remedy.”

Scene 1 — A Night at Tilly’s (Group Bar Scene)

The tavern is full. Lantern light pools over scarred wooden tables. The smell of stew and spiced mead hangs in the air. Tilly moves like a bright comet through the crowd, tankards in hand, a laugh on the tip of her tongue.

Tilly (calling out): “Right then — spilled ale makes better stories, so if you’re gonna fall, do it with flair! Who wants a refill? Who’s got a tale to trade?”

Thompson and Mildred sit side-by-side at a corner table, voices low. Thompson keeps glancing toward Mildred; she keeps hiding a smile. Tilly sidles up, elbow on the table.

Tilly (teasing): “Well, if this ain’t a sight — the smith and the potion-mistress, hearts all aflutter. Come on now, you two lovebirds, get it over with! When’s the weddin’, honeys? I’m booking the upstairs for the dancing already.”

Thompson (half-grin, sheepish): “Tilly, leave us be. You always make it sound like a show.”

Mildred (blushing): “Tilly—don’t be ridiculous.”

Tilly (dramatic whisper): “Ridiculous? Oh no, dear. This is destiny. I see it now: Thompson with soot on his hands, Mildred trailing pins of herbs and petals, Old William crying into his napkin because someone’s spending coin on flowers. You two make me feel romantic just watching.”

Across the room, a young adventurer — muddy cloak, bright eyes — clambers onto a stool, chest puffed from nerves or bravery, and calls out.

Adventurer: “Tilly! I’m heading to the Dungeon of the Vengeful Ones at dawn. Got my pack, got my blade, I’m level forty-two and—”

Tilly’s expression changes. The tavern quiets a fraction. She sets the tankard down with soft deliberation.

Tilly (soft, serious): “Love—sit down. That place chews up brave words for breakfast. My pa went down there and never came back. Donald Red Eyes didn’t come home. You heard of him?”

Adventurer (bright, defiant): “Yes. I know the stories. I can handle it.”

Tilly (leaning forward): “Handle it? What’s your gear like? What’s your plan? And—level forty-two? Bless you, but that’s not a number that walks out of there with a proper story. You need to be seasoned. I’d say, no less than sixty. Sixty. Not fifty-five, not fifty — sixty.”

There’s a murmur. From his stool, Galafe rises slowly, cane tapping the boards, his voice grave and measured.

Galafe: “Tilly’s not being dramatic. The dungeon is… old wrong. Cathedral arches, once proud; the bones within crack and grind like dry timber. Those who fall there do not stay dead as you’d wish. The Vengeful King’s necromancy is a chain; the dead rise at his will. If you fall for him, you do not sleep — you stand, you fight, you rot, and then you wake to do it again. If you love your skin, come back when you’re hardened.”

Old William (soft, heavy): “I knew a lad once who went in thinking he’d make his name. Came out a whisper. I still remember the look. Don’t make the town mend for nothing.”

Tilly (firm): “We can dress your courage in charm and stew, but we can’t stitch a soul back together. Promise me one thing — if you’re set on this, level up. Train under the guild, let Thompson temper your blade until it sings, get Mildred to brew you potions, and come back with a name and scars you’re proud of, not a tale that makes mothers weep.”

Adventurer (faltering, then angry): “You’re trying to stop me.”

Tilly (soft but hard): “I’m trying to keep you whole. If you’re so stubborn, I’ll do what I do best: sell you a charm and a stew, pocket your coin, and call you an idiot while I hope you survive. But I’ll call in Galafe to bless your steps and Old William to fix what you break on the road. That’s better than watching you become those bones.”

Galafe nods once, a sad concession; he pats the adventurer gently on the shoulder.

Galafe: “Come back to us. Live a dozen small lives rather than one endless death.”

Tension melts a bit as the adventurer sits, reconsidering. Tilly pours a glass; Thompson offers a quiet word of advice. The tavern returns to its hum — but the warning lingers like smoke in the rafters.

Scene 2 — Tilly & Thompson (Longer One-on-One)

Thompson comes to the bar after a long day at the forge, soot on his forearms. Tilly wipes a mug and smiles like she’s known him his whole life — which she has.

Tilly: “Look at you, big man — the forge wears you like a crown. Sit. I’ll put something warm in front of you.”

Thompson (settling): “Been a day, Tilly. Wheel broke, and old Mr. Carter’s plow was a mess.”

Tilly: “You always fix what’s broken. That’s why I tell everyone you’re got a heart too soft for your fists. You’re going to give a child too many sweets one day and then wonder why the whole town follows you around.”

Thompson (chuckling): “And you’re going to poke me until I do something stupid?”

Tilly (sly): “Not stupid—romantic. Tell Mildred how you feel. Don’t stutter like you’re forging a hinge. People do things with their feet sometimes—go on, Thompson. I’ll even give you the stool with the lucky scratch. It was there when I married my first true love — well, flirted with one.”

Thompson (low voice): “I’m not sure. What if she laughs?”

Tilly (leaning in): “She won’t. She’s got her own kind of courage — it shows in the way she measures her herbs. If anyone understands patience and steady hands, it’s her. You just bring the honesty. Now wipe your face, say your words straight, and I’ll ring the bell so the town hears you pledged.”

He grins despite himself. Tilly’s grin is victory enough.

Scene 3 — Tilly & Mildred (Longer One-on-One)

After minutes of Tilly’s nudging, Mildred slips into the back near the herb racks with Tilly refilling jars. The air smells of mint and lavender.

Tilly (soft, conspiratorial): “You smell of rosemary, dear. Looking lovely tonight. Tell me—when are you going to stop pretending and ask him?”

Mildred (hands fidgeting with a mortar): “Oh Tilly, you know I— I’d be foolish.”

Tilly (stern, then kind): “You’re not foolish for feeling. You’re foolish if you make a life of ‘what ifs.’ Thompson is steady; he’s honest. Give him a chance. If you need armor for your nerves, I’ll brew something. “Love potion”? No—no. But I’ll make you a calming draught and a cheering tart. Come—tomorrow morning, because you open early and he comes in early. Corner the man with his coffee and ask like you’re asking for a favor of the gods. He can say no; I’ll eat him if he does.”

Mildred (laughing through nerves): “You’re impossible, Tilly.”

Tilly (smiling): “And you’re endearing. Now go home and sleep. Dream of soft irons and strong hands.”

Scene 4 — Tilly & Elizabeth (Longer, Combative-but-Familiar)

Elizabeth comes in, all prim and parchment, eyes narrowed at the menu prices.

Elizabeth: “Tilly, that price for a room? I expected higher standards for such a steep rate.”

Tilly (tilting a mug): “Elizabeth, love. I price by warmth and character, not by ink stains. The room’s private. The bed squawks to keep thieves on their toes. If it’s your purse you’re paring, I’ll trade you an old folio for two nights, but only if the folio’s worth the nostalgia.”

Elizabeth (huffing): “I don’t need your theatrics. I have business to do.”

Tilly (mock-sad): “Oh, and miss the show? Nonsense. Look—since you’re family-adjacent, I’ll throw in hot broth and spare you my singing at three in the morning. That’s a rare discount, Elizabeth.”

Elizabeth reluctantly accepts, muttering about frivolity, while Tilly hums a tune and pockets the coin with an affectionate eye on the scribe’s softened shoulders. They bicker, but a thread of respect holds them together.

Scene 5 — Tilly & James (Kindness and Hard-won Patience)

James drifts in — hair unkempt, eyes rolling with mischief and hunger.

Tilly: “You scraped together a smile today, James? Sit. Soup’s on me. You’ll eat and then you’ll tell me one true thing you remember from the road.”

James (mumbling): “You always do…thanks.”

Tilly (handing a bowl): “I do it ‘cause someone fed me when I had nothing. Don’t waste it. You move sometimes like a ghost who forgot his own shoes. Take this, then find a day of work. I’ll give you coin if you help me clean at dawn—sweep the embers, and you’ll have a bed for the night.”

James (eyes watering): “I will. I swear it.”

Tilly (softly): “Good. Keep your promises like you keep your hat. Neither goes so far without both.”

Scene 6 — Tilly & Little Ma’ma (Gossip Trade)

Little Ma’ma leans in with a grin, fingers quick, volume higher than necessary.

Little Ma’ma: “Tilly! I heard Mr. Harrow’s son is in love with the mayor’s daughter. Tell me you heard that too.”

Tilly (pouring cider): “I did. And I heard she prefers horses to his humored speeches. Spill me what you’ve got and I’ll give you the last tart.”

Little Ma’ma (gleeful): “They were seen meeting near the orchard. He brought roses.”

Tilly (nodding): “Bribe those roses away from pigeons, love, and we’ll have ourselves a wedding that’ll empty my cellar. Keep this close, and I’ll pass on what I’ve got about the alchemist’s new herb shipment.”

They exchange gossip like old coin — favors for tidbits. Tilly always plays to win, but she gives people joy in return.

Scene 7 — Tilly & Old William (Soft Banter)

William sits at the bar with a small gemstone on the napkin. He polishes it like a man polishing memories.

Old William: “Tilly, you ever think of hanging up the apron?”

Tilly (laughing): “Old man, and give up the right to boss the world with my ladle? Never. You’d be lost without me to call you ‘Old Sparkle.’”

Old William (grinning): “You wound me and my coins.”

Tilly: “Then be careful where you hide them. You know I sell more than ale; I sell attention. For the right price, I’ll tell you where to charm a merchant.”

William chuckles. They trade jabs with affection; she slides him a bowl on the house. He pays with a story about a gem he couldn’t sell — Tilly listens like it’s the richest thing in the room.

Scene 8 — Tilly & Galafe (Serious, Respectful)

Galafe sits in his favorite corner with a candle and a pamphlet of old spells. The tavern lowers itself a notch when he speaks; his years carry weight.

Galafe: “Tilly, you handled that lad well.”

Tilly (smoothing a cloth): “I did what I could. You know how these souls are—too much courage for their boots. I thought your insight might be a weight to tip him toward sense.”

Galafe (soft): “The dungeon is not a place for heroics. The Vengeful Ones do not sleep. Their numbers swell with those who fall. The King resurrects them — binds them in his will. If someone dies there, they serve his hunger. It is not death that ends them; it is surrender.”

Tilly (quiet, steady): “You always speak the truth, old one. Keep watch on our borders, will you? Warn the ferryfolk, the wandering lads. If I can sell them a stew and a charm to keep them thinking, I will, but we need your voice too.”

Galafe: “I will speak. And if a true company forms, I will stand with them only if their wisdom outshines their bravado.”

Tilly nods, grateful — Galafe’s agreement in the bar carries a kind of spell: people listen.

Scene 9 — The Adventurer Again (Longer Exchange, Persuasion)

The adventurer rises from his stool, resolve in his jaw. He’s packed and ready, despite the warnings.

Adventurer: “I know you try to scare me, but I’ll test my fate.”

Tilly (leaning on the bar): “Listen—if you must go, do it like you plan to come back. Take Thompson’s tempering, take Mildred’s salves, learn under Galafe until he grunts at you with approval, and then set out. And if you do go now, well—leave your name with me. I’ll sing of your courage like a fool’s lullaby; if you return, I’ll buy you a stretch of pie and move heaven to make you a hero.”

Galafe (approaching): “There is rhetoric and there is counsel. If you insist on going, insure yourself with a circle of protection, and do not tarry in the open halls. The Vengeful King’s soldiers rise at intervals, and they come with memories of life and malice of death. Level matters less than preparation and unity. Go with a company, or do not go at all.”

Old William (low voice): “If I made my living off that dungeon’s bargains, I’d be a richer but sorer man. Sell charm and coin, yes — but don’t risk the life behind the coin.”

The adventurer’s shoulders slacken. He looks at the faces around him—friends, elders, lovers—and decides, with a slow breath, to be patient.

Adventurer: “All right. I’ll train. I’ll return. But when I go, I’ll make sure Vans remembers me for the right reasons.”

Tilly (grinning, raising her mug): “Then when you go—go with songs and not regrets. You’ll be fed, you’ll be outfitted, and you’ll leave with more than a blade. You’ll leave with the town’s blessing. Now, who wants to celebrate that sensible choice?”

The tavern erupts in cheers; stew and mead flow. For a night, fear is kept at bay with laughter — and Tilly’s tavern holds them all, safer under her watchful eyes.
